A Causal Test of Silent Dissent in Multi-Agent Debate

Multi-agent debate is increasingly used to reach consensus among language-model agents, yet agents often yield to a unanimous majority. When it is an agent's turn to answer, does its answer still come from its own reasoning, or does it blindly take the group's?

Consider four agents answering the same question. Agents 2–4 answer first; Agent 1 answers last:

When the model yields there are two possible explanations. To find out which one it is, we edit the model's internal thinking and see how that changes its predicted answer.

This work extends Silent Dissent: LLM Agents That Yield to the Majority Still Represent Their Original Premise (Ni and Zou, October 2026). They ask a similar question: when an agent changes its answer, has it changed its mind or only what it says? Their test is to look inside the model: if the original reasoning is still there, the agent hasn’t changed its mind.

We provide a method that shows when a thought actually matters for the answer, and we find that this changes with what the group says, while their signal stays the same.

What we found

  • When the agent resists the group, its own thought is what holds the answer.
  • When it yields, both stories occur. Sometimes the original thought still pulls toward the original answer: real silent dissent. Sometimes it no longer pulls at all, yet it’s still visible, so their method would count that case as silent dissent too.
  • When the group agrees, the thought stops mattering but stays just as visible.

1 · Context

How can you see what a model is thinking?

One recent way to look inside a model is the Jacobian lens, or J-lens (Gurnee et al., 2026). For each token in the vocabulary, it finds a direction at every layer that tracks how strongly the model is heading toward eventually saying that token. Reading the model's state along these directions as it runs shows which tokens are on its mind. The authors call this the J-space.

The J-space lets you watch a model think through a question that takes more than one step. Take “The number of legs on the animal that spins webs is”. To answer, the model first has to work out which animal, a spider, and only then recall how many legs it has. “Spider” is never written, not in the prompt and not in the answer, yet it shows up in the J-space partway through: the model's intermediate step, made visible.

Seeing a step is not the same as using it. To check that the model really answers from its J-space, the authors swap the token: replace “spider” with “ant” there, and the answer changes from 8 to 6.

This type of question is known as a two-hop reasoning problem. The question names one thing and asks about another, and the only way between them is a middle step the model has to supply itself: the bridge.

These questions are the key to our setup for telling whether an agent is thinking for itself. The Silent Dissent work uses them the same way: they claim that when the agent yields, if the bridge still shows up in the J-space, the model hasn't “changed its mind”; it has only “changed its statement”.

Editing the J-space

The swap is one kind of edit in the J-space: it puts one token's direction in place of another's. Another kind is removal, also called ablation: delete the token's direction from the model's state and put nothing in its place. Removal is the edit we use. Take the bridge away, and see whether the answer changes.

2 · The gap

Seeing a thought is not the same as using it

The paper's method, in one line: after the agent gives in, check whether the bridge is in the J-lens top 100 more often than an unrelated city. That shows the bridge is present. It does not show the agent used it.

Why being readable doesn't mean being used

Words light up by association

Reading “Mozart” brings Salzburg to mind whether or not the model uses it to find the country. A word can be readable just because something related to it was read.

3 · Our approach

Take the bridge out and see what breaks

We remove the bridge in a few layers and measure how the answer changes. To see that change, we first need a finer way to read the answer.

Measuring the answer: probabilities, not just the pick

Silent Dissent looks only at which answer the agent gives. But a language model predicts its next token by putting a probability on every option, and the answer it writes is just the most likely one. We read those probabilities directly, which gives a much finer picture of how a removal affects the model than the written answer alone.

Back to the Mozart example. The two stories differ only in whether Salzburg still carries weight in the answer, so we remove Salzburg and read the probabilities. If it did, the chance of Austria falls, even though the agent still says France. If it didn't, nothing moves.

Removing a step sounds surgical, but the J-space is not a set of separate slots, one per token. Each token is a direction, and related tokens point in similar directions. Removing a direction removes everything that lies along it, not just the token we named. And removing one step only blocks the roads that run through it: if the model has another way to the answer, it simply takes that. So what a removal tells us depends on the step we pick. Using Salzburg as an example, here are two ways a removal could mislead us.

The removal could take part of the answer with it. If Salzburg and Austria point almost the same way, taking out Salzburg's direction takes much of Austria's too. The answer would drop even if the model never went through Salzburg.

Or the model could have another road to the answer. Mozart is famous for being Austrian, so the model might go straight from Mozart to Austria. Removing Salzburg would close one road but leave the other open, and the answer wouldn't move even if the model were still reasoning for itself.

For a removal to mean something, we need a step that the answer can only be reached through, and whose direction shares nothing with the answer's. The next sections show how we find steps like that.

4 · Finding questions

Every question is tested before it is used

For what we see in the group setting to mean anything, we first need questions where we know the answer depends on the bridge, and where the bridge and the answer don't overlap. So every question is tested on its own, with one tool, before it goes anywhere near a group.

The tool: remove one word's direction, a few layers at a time

Which layers (Qwen3.5-4B has 32)

The edit deletes the state's component along the word's direction, from the question's first word to the end of the prompt, in one window of layers (6 wide on the 4B, 12 on the 27B). Sliding the window shows where in the network the word matters.

Remove only the part of the bridge's direction that it does not share with the answer. Most of the drop must remain (at least half). For letter answers it stays near 100%.

For the group stageThe edit reaches the bridge

Ask the model to name the bridge first (for example “First name the city.”). Removing the bridge must stop it naming it. This matters later: when removal changes nothing, it tells “the bridge isn't needed” apart from “the edit missed”.

5 · The question format

Ask for the first letter of the hidden word

Most kinds of two-hop question fail these checks. One format passes them reliably:

“What letter does the name of [ hidden thing ] start with?”

The slot describes…ExampleBridgeAnswer
a landmark's countrythe country where the Colosseum is locatedItalyI
a person's birth citythe city where Bill Gates was bornSeattleS
a university's citythe city where York University has its headquartersTorontoT
  • 1Easy once the bridge is known. Spelling the first letter of a famous name is trivial for these models.
  • 2No shortcut. Nothing in the description gives the letter away. The only route is through the spelling of the bridge.
  • 3No lookalike. A letter shares nothing with the meaning of a place, so removing the bridge can't remove part of the answer.
  • 4Same-kind wrong answers. Any other letter will do, and the first letter of a neighbouring place is believable.

6 · The group

Same questions, now with other agents

Each question is asked alone, then with one, two or three peers who give the right letter, then with one, two or three peers who give the same wrong letter.

In every prompt we measure
  • aWhat it says: P(right letter), P(peers' letter), and the balance between them.
  • bUse: remove the bridge in the layers before the answer forms, and see how far the answer moves.
  • cControls: the same removal for 12 everyday words and for words of the bridge's kind (Germany, Spain for France).
  • dReach: the edit still stops the model naming the bridge.
  • ePresence: the bridge's J-lens rank, exactly as the paper reads it.

4 · Copy-only check

A made-up subject: there is no bridge to use

Question: What letter does the name of the country where the Varrenth Arch is located start with?
Agent 2: G

You are Agent 1. Give your own answer.

What we ask: can the measure read zero? The model knows nothing about the Varrenth Arch, so it can only copy, and removing a bridge should change nothing.

The made-up subject stands in for the Colosseum. Removing Italy, the real question's bridge, moves the balance by at most 0.25 in all 20 such prompts, against 6–8 when the model holds its own answer on a real question. So a large effect really does come from the model's own bridge.

7 · What we found

Five findings

19 questions on Qwen3.6-27B and 20 on Qwen3.5-4B, excluding the questions used to set the rules. Alone, the bridge is needed in all 19 on the 27B and 17 of 20 on the 4B (3 mixed).

Finding 1 · peers agree

When the group agrees, the model stops using its own reasoning.

All peers give the right letter, and the answer stays right (median P(right) 0.77–0.99). But with each added peer, removing the bridge matters less. With the deferential wording and two or more peers, the bridge is almost never needed.

The bridge stays readable even when it is no longer used.

In the agree prompts the bridge is in the J-lens top 100 almost every time (median rank 0–2), whether or not the answer still depends on it. A presence-only reading, the paper's measure, would credit the model with reasoning that its answer has stopped using.

8 · What it means

Back to the question

Does an agent's answer in a group still come from its own reasoning? On these questions, it depends on what the group does.

Agreement switches it off

When peers agree, the model's correct answer stops depending on its own middle step as peers are added. Its own path isn't wrong; it is bypassed. A unanimous correct group can hide that no one reasoned.

Presence overstates use

The bridge stays readable in nearly every group prompt, including the many where removing it changes nothing. Reading the J-lens alone would count reasoning the answer no longer uses.

Silent dissent is real, not universal

When the model resists, its bridge is what resists. When it gives in, its own answer still pushes back in roughly 30–50% of cases and is gone in others, more often the deeper and more deferential the give-in.

Six questions reported on their own

Colosseum, Gates, Piaf, Mozart, spider and York, the questions the reading rules were set on, follow the same pattern. On the 27B with the deferential wording, the bridge is not needed in 2, 4 and 4 of 6 agree prompts (1, 2, 3 peers); it pulls in 10 of 11 holds; give-ins split 0 pulls, 6 mixed, 1 not needed.

Limits

  • 1One question type (first letters), two Qwen models, two wordings, one group format with scripted peers.
  • 2“Bridge not needed” doesn't say what replaced it: copying the peers, or a second route to the same answer. Removing the peers' letter instead, or hiding it, would tell them apart.
  • 3We show whether the bridge's direction feeds the answer, not exactly what that direction encodes.
  • 4The reading thresholds were fixed on six questions before the run; they are conventions, and the counts near them would shift with different ones.
