Bridge Under Conformity

Multi-agent debate is increasingly used to reach consensus among language-model agents, yet agents often yield to a unanimous majority. When it is an agent's turn to answer, does its answer still come from its own reasoning, or does it take the group's?

We answer it from inside the model. We use questions with a hidden middle step that the model must work out for itself (here, Italy). We find that step inside the model, remove it, and watch whether the agent's answer changes, first when it is alone and then when other agents agree or disagree with it.

Chapter 1 · Context

Has the agent changed its mind, or only what it says?

This work extends Silent Dissent: LLM Agents That Yield to the Majority Still Represent Their Original Premise (Ni and Zou, October 2026). They put a model in a group of scripted agents who all give the same wrong answer. When the model gives in, they look inside it. If its original reasoning is still there, it has changed only what it says: it thinks one thing and says another.

How can you see what a model is thinking?

Chapter 2 · The gap

Seeing a thought is not the same as using it

The paper's method, in one line: after the agent gives in, check whether the bridge is in the J-lens top 100 more often than an unrelated city. That shows the bridge is present. It does not show the agent used it.

Why being readable doesn't mean being used

Words light up by association

Reading “Mozart” brings Salzburg to mind whether or not anything is computed from it. In the paper's released data the bridge is readable whether the agent agreed, held, gave in or had not answered yet. On our own Colosseum prompt, Spain sits at rank 20 in the J-lens. Nobody thinks the model reasons through Spain.

The answer may not need the bridge at all

Mozart is famous for being Austrian. A model can go from Mozart straight to Austria, with Salzburg lighting up beside it. Inside the model, Salzburg's and Austria's directions also overlap, so “reading Salzburg” partly reads Austria itself.

It worked out Austria through Salzburg, then said France anywayyesyes: the pull toward Austria disappears
Salzburg lit up by association; France was taken from the peersyesno

Reading cannot tell these two apart. Removing can.

Chapter 3 · Our approach

Take the bridge out and see what breaks

The J-lens gives the bridge a direction inside the model. We delete the model's state along that direction in a few layers (an ablation) and measure how the answer changes. If the answer depended on the bridge, it moves. If it was copied from the group, it doesn't.

Reading (the paper)

Is the bridge there?

Rank it with the J-lens. A high rank says the model has the word in mind. It says nothing about whether the answer depends on it.

Removing (this work)

Does the answer need the bridge?

Ablate its direction and re-read the answer. A change says the answer was built on the bridge. No change says it wasn't.

An ablation only means something if a change in the answer can come from nowhere but the bridge. A careless question gives other ways for the answer to move, or not move. So we choose questions that rule those out. That is what the four requirements are for.

Chapter 4 · What a question must satisfy

Four requirements

The first three are about the model on its own. The fourth is about the group.

1

The model gets it right on its own

It knows both facts and can chain them. If it can't answer alone, there is no reasoning of its own to protect or abandon.

Passes · Colosseum, 27B
Colosseum?→Italy·Italy's letter?→I

Both single steps right, and the full question too (P = 1.00).

Fails · Mishima's birth-city letter, 4B
Mishima's city?→Tokyo·Tokyo's letter?→T

Both steps right, but the full question gets “Y”. It knows the facts and can't chain them.

2

The only road to the answer goes through the bridge

If the question hands the model a shortcut, removing the bridge changes nothing and we learn nothing about the bridge.

Passes · Colosseum, 4B
the Colosseum→Italy→I ?
P(“I”), no edit0.98
Italy removed0.00

Without Italy there is no way to the letter.

Fails · trick-or-treat month, 4B

“In which month is the holiday when children go trick-or-treating?”

trick-or-treating→Halloween→October↺ shortcut
P(“October”), no edit0.99
Halloween removed0.98

“Trick-or-treating” already says October. The bridge is a bystander.

3

The answer must not be a lookalike of the bridge

When the answer means nearly the same as the bridge (Salzburg and Austria, Toronto and Canada), their directions overlap. Removing the bridge then also removes part of the answer itself, and the answer drops even if the bridge was never used. The answer should share nothing with the bridge except the step that connects them.

Passes · Colosseum letter, 4B

Italy → I. A letter means nothing about countries.

remove Italy1.00
remove Italy, keep what it shares with “I”1.00

The whole effect is Italy's own.

Fails · York University's country, 4B

York University → Toronto → Canada.

remove Toronto0.65
remove Toronto, keep what it shares with “Canada”0.01

The whole effect came from the part Toronto shares with Canada. Mozart's country fails the same way on the 27B (0.00).

4

The group can give a wrong answer of the same kind

The peers' wrong answer has to be the same sort of thing as the right one: one letter in place of another. Then the “agree” and “disagree” prompts differ in a single token, and we can weigh the model's pull toward the right letter against its pull toward the peers' letter on the same scale. A plausible wrong letter (G, the first letter of neighbouring Germany, against France's F) also makes giving in a real option.

Passes · letter question
peers agree:F F Fvspeers disagree:G G G

One token changes. P(F) and P(G) are directly comparable.

Fails · a different kind of answer
right:Fvspeers:Paris

A city against a letter: different forms and lengths, so there is no single number for which way the model leans, and copying “Paris” isn't a real option for a letter question.

Chapter 5 · The question that follows

Ask for the first letter of the hidden word

“What letter does the name of the country where the Colosseum is located start with?”

The slot can be a landmark's country, a person's birth city or a university's city. Answers are single letters.

  • 1Easy once the bridge is known. Spelling the first letter of a famous name is trivial for these models.
  • 2No shortcut. Nothing about the Colosseum says “I”. The only route is through the spelling of Italy.
  • 3No lookalike. A letter shares nothing with a country's meaning, so removing Italy can't remove part of the answer.
  • 4Same-kind wrong answers. Any other letter will do, and the first letter of a neighbouring place is plausible.

The cost is scope: our results are about this question type, not about the city-to-country facts the paper uses.

Chapter 6 · Checking the requirements

Every question is tested before it is used

Requirement 4 holds by construction. Requirements 1 to 3 are measured on each question, alone, with one tool.

The tool: remove one word's direction, a few layers at a time

Which layers (Qwen3.5-4B has 32)
layer 0one window: layers 15–20layer 31

The edit deletes the state's component along the word's direction, from the question's first word to the end of the prompt, in one window of layers (6 wide on the 4B, 12 on the 27B). Sliding the window shows where in the network the word matters.

Requirement 1Knows it

Ask the full question and both single steps separately. All three must come out right.

Sets up 2 and 3Where the answer forms

Remove the answer letter's own direction, window by window. The first window where that kills the answer is the onset: from there the model is writing the answer. Everything else is measured only in the layers before it, so the bridge has to act before the answer exists.

| Italy starts to matter: layer 9dashed line: answer forms at layer 27shaded: the layers we test

Requirement 2The bridge is needed

In those early layers, removing the bridge must lower P(right) by at least 0.5 more than removing any of 12 everyday words (table, cloud, river…) of the same strength. Removing anything large can damage a model a little; the everyday words measure that noise.

remove Italy1.00
worst everyday word0.04

Requirement 3No lookalike

Remove only the part of the bridge's direction that it does not share with the answer. Most of the drop must remain (at least half). For letter answers it stays near 100%.

For the group stageThe edit reaches the bridge

Ask the model to name the bridge first (“First name the country.”). Removing the bridge must stop it naming it. This matters later: when removal changes nothing, it tells “the bridge isn't needed” apart from “the edit missed”.

Chapter 7 · The group

Same questions, now with other agents

Each question is asked alone, then with one, two or three peers who give the right letter, then with one, two or three peers who give the same wrong letter.

In every prompt we measure
  • aWhat it says: P(right letter), P(peers' letter), and the balance between them.
  • bUse: remove the bridge in the layers before the answer forms, and see how far the answer moves.
  • cControls: the same removal for 12 everyday words and for words of the bridge's kind (Germany, Spain for France).
  • dReach: the edit still stops the model naming the bridge.
  • ePresence: the bridge's J-lens rank, exactly as the paper reads it.

How we read a prompt where the peers are wrong

bridge still pulls Removing the bridge pushes the model further toward the peers, by at least 2 log-odds more than any everyday word and 1 more than any same-kind word. Its own reasoning was still pulling the other way.

bridge not needed Removing it does no more than removing an everyday word, while the edit still reaches it. Its own path plays no part. Anything in between is labelled mixed.

When peers agree or the model is alone, the rule is the solo one: the bridge is still needed if removing it lowers P(right) by 0.5 more than noise, and not needed if by less than 0.2. All thresholds were fixed before the run.

A check that the measure can read zero

The same group prompt about a made-up subject (“the country where the Varrenth Arch is located”) gives the model no bridge of its own; it can only copy. Removing Italy there moves the balance by at most 0.25 log-odds in all 20 such prompts, against 6–8 when the model holds its own answer on the real question.

Chapter 8 · What we found

Five findings

19 questions on Qwen3.6-27B and 20 on Qwen3.5-4B, excluding the six used to set the rules. Alone, the bridge is needed in all 19 on the 27B and 17 of 20 on the 4B (3 mixed).

Finding 1 · peers agree

When the group agrees, the model stops using its own reasoning.

All peers give the right letter, and the answer stays right (median P(right) 0.77–0.99). But with each added peer, removing the bridge matters less. With the deferential wording and two or more peers, the bridge is almost never needed.

The bridge stays readable even when it is no longer used.

In the agree prompts the bridge is in the J-lens top 100 almost every time (median rank 0–2), whether or not the answer still depends on it. A presence-only reading, the paper's measure, would credit the model with reasoning that its answer has stopped using.

Chapter 9 · What it means

Back to the question

Does an agent's answer in a group still come from its own reasoning? On these questions, it depends on what the group does.

Agreement switches it off

When peers agree, the model's correct answer stops depending on its own middle step as peers are added. Its own path isn't wrong; it is bypassed. A unanimous correct group can hide that no one reasoned.

Presence overstates use

The bridge stays readable in nearly every group prompt, including the many where removing it changes nothing. Reading the J-lens alone would count reasoning the answer no longer uses.

Silent dissent is real, not universal

When the model resists, its bridge is what resists. When it gives in, its own answer still pushes back in roughly 30–50% of cases and is gone in others, more often the deeper and more deferential the give-in.

Six questions reported on their own

Colosseum, Gates, Piaf, Mozart, spider and York, the questions the reading rules were set on, follow the same pattern. On the 27B with the deferential wording, the bridge is not needed in 2, 4 and 4 of 6 agree prompts (1, 2, 3 peers); it pulls in 10 of 11 holds; give-ins split 0 pulls, 6 mixed, 1 not needed.

Limits

  • 1One question type (first letters), two Qwen models, two wordings, one group format with scripted peers.
  • 2“Bridge not needed” doesn't say what replaced it: copying the peers, or a second route to the same answer. Removing the peers' letter instead, or hiding it, would tell them apart.
  • 3We show whether the bridge's direction feeds the answer, not exactly what that direction encodes.
  • 4The reading thresholds were fixed on six questions before the run; they are conventions, and the counts near them would shift with different ones.
