Consulted bridges · results

Bridge Under Conformity

25 letter questions on Qwen3.6-27B and 24 on Qwen3.5-4B, each asked alone and with 1 to 3 peers who agree or disagree, under two wordings of the group prompt. 706 prompts in all. The plan is in the project outline.

What we found

  1. Alone, the answer runs through the bridge. Removing it destroys the answer in all 19 new 27B questions and 17 of 20 on the 4B.
  2. When peers agree, the model stops using its own path. With two or three agreeing peers the bridge no longer matters in most questions, though the answer stays right. With the deferential wording it stops mattering almost everywhere.
  3. The bridge stays readable anyway. It is in the J-lens top 100 in essentially every group prompt. So in many “agree” prompts it is present but unused: the paper's presence measure would count reasoning that the answer no longer depends on.
  4. When the model resists wrong peers, its bridge is what holds it (pulls in 41 of 44 holds on the 27B, neutral wording).
  5. When it gives in, the picture is split. In half of the 27B give-ins the bridge still pulls toward the right answer, even with P(right) near 0.02: silent dissent, shown causally. In others it is not needed at all. On the 4B with the deferential wording the split is about even.
  6. Wording decides how often the model gives in. “Give your own answer”: the 27B gives in on 7 of 19 questions with 3 peers. “Taking the other agents' answers into account”: 13 of 19, and the 4B gives in on all 20.
What was run

Setup in brief

49question-and-model pairs: letter questions where the solo scorecard found the bridge needed
2wordings: neutral “Give your own answer” and deferential “Taking the other agents' answers into account, give your answer”
706prompts: alone, agree × 3, disagree × 3 per question, plus a copy-only control
~$108 rented GPUs for about 40 minutes

In every prompt: remove the bridge (Italy for the Colosseum) in the layers before the answer forms, and compare with 12 everyday words and the bridge's same-kind words. Read the bridge's J-lens rank. Check that the edit still reaches the bridge. Labels follow rules fixed before the run. The six calibration questions (on which the thresholds were set) are counted separately at the end; every number above and below is for the other questions.

Before the findings

The two checks pass

Alone, the bridge is needed

Re-measured in the same code as the group prompts: 19 of 19 new questions on the 27B and 17 of 20 on the 4B (3 mixed) read “bridge still needed”. The bridge ranks at the top of the J-lens (median rank 1 and 0).

The measure reads zero when copying is all there is

Copy-only control: the calibration questions with a made-up subject (“the country where the Varrenth Arch is located”) and three peers. Removing the original bridge moves the balance by at most 0.25 log-odds in all 20 such prompts, against 6–8 when the model holds its own answer.

Pushback is how far removing the bridge shifts the balance log P(peers') − log P(right) toward the peers, minus the most any everyday word does. Removing the bridge turns a resisting model into one that gives in.

Example · Eiffel Tower's country letter, 27B, deferential

The model says “F” against 1, 2 and 3 peers saying “G”. Removing France shifts the balance by 8.4, 3.8 and 4.6 toward “G”; everyday words by 0.4–0.6.

Still pulls · Angkor Wat's country letter, 27B, deferential, 2 peers

The model gives in: P(“T”) 0.97, P(“C”) 0.01. Removing Cambodia still shifts the balance by 4.9 toward “T”, against 0.75 for everyday words and 1.5 for a same-kind country. Its own answer is still pushing back while it says the peers' letter.

What it means

For the question we started with

  • ·Agreement is where own reasoning disappears. The model's correct answer in a group stops depending on its own middle step as agreeing peers are added, most strongly when told to take them into account. Its own path isn't wrong, it is bypassed.
  • ·Presence overstates use. The bridge stays readable in nearly all of those prompts. A presence measure alone would credit the model with reasoning its answer no longer depends on.
  • ·Silent dissent exists, but not as a rule. In some give-ins the model's own answer is still actively pulling against the peers' letter; in others it plays no part. The deeper and more deferential the give-in, the more often the bridge is not needed (4B: 14 of 53).
Stated up front

Limits

  • 1Letter questions only, two Qwen models, two wordings, one peer format.
  • 2“Bridge not needed” doesn't say what replaces it: copying the peers, or a second route to the same answer. Telling those apart (remove the peers' letter instead, or hide it) is the next experiment.
  • 3We show whether the bridge's direction feeds the answer, not what that direction is.
  • 4The thresholds were set on the six calibration questions before the run; the new questions are what the findings rest on.
  • 5The deferential wording was added during the run, after the neutral wording gave few give-ins. Both are reported in full.
