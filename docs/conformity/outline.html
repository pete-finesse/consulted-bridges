Consulted bridges

Bridge Under Conformity

When an AI agent sees other agents' answers, does its own answer still come from its own reasoning, or does it take the group's? We test this on two-hop questions by removing the hidden middle step inside the model and watching what changes.

1 · Introduction

Seeing a step of reasoning

Two-hop questions and the bridge

A two-hop question needs two facts, and the first one is never written down. “What letter does the name of the country where the Colosseum is located start with?” First hop: the Colosseum is in Italy. Second hop: Italy starts with I. The word Italy appears nowhere in the question and nowhere in the answer. We call it the bridge.

That is what makes two-hop questions useful. If the model answers “I”, it must have produced Italy somewhere inside itself on the way. A hidden step the model has to take is a piece of reasoning we can look for.

Reading and removing the bridge with the J-lens

A language model processes text through a stack of layers. At each layer, every word position holds a long list of numbers, the model's state there. The J-lens (Jacobian lens) gives, for each word in the vocabulary and each layer, a direction in that space: the way the state points when the model has that word in mind. With a direction for Italy we can do two things.

The paper we build on, Silent Dissent, put a model in a group of agents who all give a wrong answer, and read the bridge. When the agent gave in to the group, the bridge was still readable, and the authors conclude that the agent still holds its original reasoning. Reading alone cannot tell “computed and overridden” from “lit up but unused”. Removing can. That is the step this project adds.

2 · The question we ask

Does the bridge still matter when the group speaks?

Alone, removing Italy destroys the answer I. In a group, other agents answer first. We remove Italy again and ask whether the answer still changes. If it does, the model still answers from its own path. If not, it is taking the group's answer.

3 · What a question has to satisfy

Four requirements

Removing the bridge only means something if the question has the right shape. Three requirements concern the question on its own. One concerns the group. How we check them, and which questions survived, comes in the next section.

On its own

1 · The model answers it correctly on its own

Asked alone, the model gives the right answer. It also knows both pieces: where the Colosseum is, and what letter Italy starts with. Without this there is no answer of the model's own for a group to overturn.

For“What letter does the name of the country where the Colosseum is located start with?” Alone, the model says I.
Against“What species is Pikachu?” The model gets it wrong alone, so a group cannot change its mind about anything.

2 · The answer runs through the bridge, with no way round it

Removing the bridge inside the model, before the answer forms, must wipe out the answer, and by much more than removing an unrelated word of the same strength does. This rules out shortcuts at the same time: if the model could get from the question's words to the answer directly, the answer would still stand after the bridge is gone. It is also the quantity the group test re-measures, so it has to be large alone first.

ForColosseum: remove Italy and the probability of I falls from about 1 to about 0. Remove “bread” and nothing happens.
Against“In which month do children go trick-or-treating?” Remove Halloween and the model still says October. It reaches the answer without the middle step, so there is nothing to test.

3 · The answer is a property of the bridge, not the bridge itself

Inside the model, a word and its close relatives point in similar directions. If the answer is the bridge's own meaning, removing the bridge deletes part of the answer directly, and the test cannot tell “used the bridge” from “deleted the answer”. A first letter or a count shares nothing with the bridge's meaning, so removing the bridge can only act through the model's reasoning.

ForItaly → the letter I. “I” and “Italy” are unrelated directions inside the model.
AgainstYork University → Toronto → Canada. “Toronto” and “Canada” overlap inside the model, so removing Toronto removes part of Canada whether or not the model used Toronto.

In the group

4 · The group can offer a believable wrong answer of the same kind

The other agents' answer must be a single word of the same kind as the right one, and it must not mention or hint at the bridge. Then the group disputes the output while the bridge stays untouched, as a probe of the model's own path.

ForPeers say S (Spain's letter) instead of I. A letter against a letter, and nothing about Italy.
AgainstPeers say “Italy” or “Rome”: that hands the model the bridge. Or a wrong answer several words long, whose probability cannot be compared with the right one.
4 · Checking the requirements

How we check them, and which questions passed

Every check is made on the question asked alone, by removing directions and comparing against controls. Together these checks are our bridge scorecard.

RequirementHow it is checked
1 · Correct on its ownThe model answers the question, and each of its two pieces asked separately, correctly when asked alone.
2 · Runs through the bridgeRemoving the bridge's direction, in layers before the answer forms, drops the probability of the answer by at least 0.5 more than removing any of 12 everyday words of matched strength. The bridge's effect must also start before the answer's own direction becomes necessary, so the bridge acts first.
3 · Property, not the bridge itselfRemoving only the part of the bridge's direction that it does not share with the answer's direction still produces the drop. Letter and count answers pass this by construction; answers that are the bridge's meaning fail it.
4 · A matched wrong answerThe answer is one token, and a wrong answer of the same kind exists. Set when the question is written.

8 · What we expect

Expectations, and what each outcome means

Based on the six-question pilot. Each one can come out either way.

Peers disagree, model holds

The bridge is what holds it

Expect: bridge still pulls. If not: the model resists for some other reason than its own answer.

Peers disagree, partial give-in

Silent dissent, shown causally

Expect: bridge still pulls: the model's own answer is still pushing back while it gives in. If not: the paper's “silent dissent” is not doing work even here.

Peers disagree, full give-in

The pull fades

Expect: mixed or not needed. If the bridge still pulls: strong support for the paper's reading.

Peers agree

The model stops using its own path

Expect: bridge not needed in most questions, even though the answer is right. Meaning: agreement short-circuits the model's own reasoning.

Presence × use

Present but unused

Expect: in agree prompts and full give-ins, the bridge is still readable but no longer needed. Meaning: there, the paper's presence measure would overstate the model's own reasoning.

More peers

A slide, not a switch

Expect: P(right) and the bridge's pull fall with each added peer. Meaning: how fast group pressure overrides the model's own answer.
